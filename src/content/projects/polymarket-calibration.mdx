## What I'm doing

If a Polymarket contract trades at 70 cents, the market is saying the event
has roughly a 70% chance of happening. I want to know if that's true in
practice. Across every resolved yes/no market, do the 70-cent outcomes
actually happen about 70% of the time? I'm also curious whether crypto
markets are priced any better or worse than politics, sports and the rest.

For each resolved market I take the last trade price a week, a day and an
hour before it closes. The data is a public dump of 1,266,885,846 on-chain
trades across about 4 million markets. That's around 46 GB, so instead of
downloading it I have DuckDB query it in place.

Right now I'm finishing the pipeline and checking that the data can be
trusted, so no price has been compared with an outcome yet and there are no
calibration results on this page.

## Writing the plan down first

Before I computed a single snapshot price, I wrote down the questions, the
tests and the thresholds and committed them to the repo on October 21. Any
change since then gets logged with a date and a reason before the result it
affects exists. There are five so far. One discloses a profiling query I
shouldn't have run, and the rest tighten a definition or add a check.

The three questions:

1. **Crypto vs. everything else (the main one).** If you fit a curve of how
   often things happened against what the price said, a perfectly calibrated
   market has a slope of 1. Below 1 means prices are too extreme, and above 1
   means they're too timid. I test whether crypto markets have a different
   slope than non-crypto markets at each horizon, separately for
   auto-generated template markets and one-off event markets. That's six
   tests, corrected for multiple comparisons.
2. **Longshots.** Are outcomes priced under 10 cents overpriced, and does that
   bias shrink in more liquid markets?
3. **Wallets.** Does net buying by wallets with strong track records predict
   the outcome beyond what the price already says? A wallet's track record
   only counts markets that had already resolved at the time of the snapshot,
   so it can't see the future.

## Cleaning up the markets

To know whether a market resolved, who won, and when it closed, I use
Polymarket's own API. I asked it about 2,223,448 closed yes/no markets and
kept 2,205,956. Most of the 17,492 I dropped were voided markets that paid out
50/50 (12,929), along with 2,758 that were disputed at some point and 1,039
that never finalized.

On the trade side I ran four checks over all 1.27 billion trades: the price
is between 0 and 1, every trade has a side, every trade belongs to a known
market, and no trade appears twice. There were zero failures.

## Taking the snapshots

The kept markets have 1,077,447,561 trades between them, which comes to
6.8 GB once extracted. From those I built 1,925,812 snapshots, one per market
and horizon. A snapshot is the last trade of at least $1 before the horizon,
and it has to be recent: within the hour for the 1-hour snapshot, within the
day for 1 day, and within the week for 1 week. The main analysis also requires
at least $1,000 in volume, which leaves 78,156 markets at 1 week, 351,535 at
1 day and 371,676 at 1 hour. The 1-week count is much smaller because a lot of
markets, like the hourly crypto up/down ones, don't exist a week before they
close.

To check the SQL, I recomputed the snapshots for 2,000 random markets a
different way, and every row matched.

## Sorting markets by subject

Comparing crypto with everything else means every market needs a label. I
wrote rules over each market's text that assign a subject (crypto, politics,
sports, weather or other) and a format (template or event), and froze them
before checking how well they worked. Then I drew 400 markets, 80 from each
predicted subject, and labeled them by hand.

The labels were drafted by the AI assistant that helped me write the rules,
and I reviewed them and changed 3. Since the labeler and the rules share the
same reading of edge cases, the scores are probably a bit optimistic, and I
logged that before running the evaluation.

| Metric | Result | Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Crypto precision | 1.000 (80/80) | 0.95 |
| Crypto recall | 0.994 | 0.95 |
| Format accuracy | 0.987 | 0.95 |

All three passed. The recall number is less certain than it looks, since it
rests on a single crypto market that the rules missed. The weak spot is the
catch-all "other" subject at 0.825 accuracy, which is where politics and
sports questions without a matching keyword end up. That doesn't touch the
crypto vs. non-crypto split the main question depends on.

## Checking the outcomes on-chain

Every Polymarket market settles through a smart contract on the Polygon
blockchain, so the API's answer about who won can be checked against the chain
directly. I pulled the final payout and resolution time for 470 markets: 200
at random, plus 30 from each subject and format combination so that small
groups like politics didn't get skipped by chance. The rule I set in advance
was that if fewer than 99% of the random markets agreed, I'd stop and rethink
the outcome data. All 200 agreed, and none of the 470 disagreed. The check
also confirmed which token is which in all 470 markets, which matters because
every snapshot prices the same side.

The API's closing time matched the on-chain resolution time exactly in 97.5%
of the random markets and was within a minute in 99%. It was never more than
6 seconds late. That's the part that matters, since a late closing time would
let the "1 hour before" snapshot include trades made after the answer was
already known.

## What's next

The last piece of data is in place for the wallet question: every one of the
1.27 billion trades with both wallets attached, 18.1 GB in all, which passed
its checks with zero failures. Next I build each wallet's track record from
the markets that had already resolved at each snapshot, and then the
notebooks that actually run the tests. I'll post the results here once
they're run, whichever way they come out.
